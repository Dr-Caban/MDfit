The Iliopsoas Complex

Anatomy · Attachments · Pain Patterns · Clinical Insights

Strong Hips. Solid Foundation.

The iliopsoas consists of the psoas major and iliacus — two muscles that merge into the body's primary hip flexor. It connects the lumbar spine to the femur, bridging the trunk and legs. When tight or dysfunctional, it contributes to low back pain, groin pain, and hip tightness.

  • Primary hip flexor of the body
  • Bridges the spine to the leg
  • Major contributor to low back pain
  • Shortens with prolonged sitting
  • Often the most undertreated muscle

Anatomy

The Muscles

Psoas Major

  • Origin: T12–L5 vertebral bodies and discs
  • Insertion: Lesser trochanter of the femur
  • Function: Hip flexion, lumbar stabilization, postural alignment
  • Common Issues: Low back pain, anterior pelvic tilt, lumbar lordosis

Iliacus

  • Origin: Iliac fossa (inner surface of pelvis)
  • Insertion: Lesser trochanter of the femur
  • Function: Hip flexion, stabilization of the hip joint
  • Common Issues: Groin pain, hip tightness, inner thigh referral

Referred Pain Patterns

Psoas Major

  • Low back pain parallel to the spine (L1–L5)
  • Groin pain (deep, aching)
  • Front of thigh pain
  • Can mimic disc pathology or nerve root compression

Iliacus

  • Groin pain and front of hip
  • Inner thigh referral
  • Back of pelvis and high glute
  • Often more symptomatic than psoas in clinical practice

Clinical Pearls

  • The iliopsoas is the most undertreated muscle in the body.
  • It sits behind the abdominal organs, so it's missed on standard exam.
  • Prolonged sitting shortens it — the modern epidemic.
  • A tight psoas pulls the lumbar spine into lordosis and tilts the pelvis forward.
  • The iliacus is often more symptomatic than the psoas in clinical practice.
  • Always assess hip extension range and Thomas test.

The Takeaway

  • The iliopsoas connects your spine to your legs — it affects everything.
  • Low back pain, groin pain, and hip tightness often start here.
  • Prolonged sitting is the number one risk factor.
  • Release, mobilize, and strengthen for lasting relief.
